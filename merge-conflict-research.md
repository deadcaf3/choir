# Solving the Merge-Conflict Resolution Ceiling for Agent-Scale Software Collaboration

## TL;DR
- The <60% ceiling on real-conflict resolution is real and confirmed by 2026 evidence (Merge-Bench: Gemini 2.5 Pro 54.7% textual / 62.5% normalized-code match; the RL-trained 14B LLMergeJ 48.8%/58.9%), but **it is the wrong number to optimize**. The maximum *safe* automation level today is roughly **40-55% of conflicts auto-resolved at very high precision** via a **calibrated-abstention + verification** stack, with the remainder escalated.
- The single highest-leverage move is to reframe this from an *accuracy* problem into a **selective-prediction problem**: an LLM-as-judge validated at **100% precision / 64.6% recall** composed with a solver, gated by **post-merge differential testing**, converts a ~55%-accurate tool into a near-zero-false-accept auto-merger that abstains on the rest.
- The single advance that would raise the safe ceiling most is a **calibrated semantic-conflict detector** (code that compiles but breaks behavior) with a provable low false-negative rate — because verification, not generation, is the binding constraint once prevention and structured merge have dissolved the easy conflicts.

## Key Findings

1. **Nobody has credibly beaten 60% on real conflicts.** The best 2026 result, Gemini 2.5 Pro on Merge-Bench (7,938 hunks from 1,439 repositories), is 54.7% textual / 62.5% normalized-code match; the purpose-trained 14B RL model LLMergeJ hits 48.8%/58.9%. Schesch & Ernst state plainly: *"Across 11 programming languages, commercial LLM performance is largely stable from language to language. The best models correctly resolve less than 60% of merge conflicts."* These are exact/source-match metrics that *understate* true correctness, but the ceiling holds.
2. **Abstention changes the shape of the problem.** Traditional structured tools already abstain (leave conflict markers) — their value is precision-when-attempted, not coverage. The frontier is composing a high-recall solver with a high-precision judge so the *attempted* set is resolved near-perfectly and the rest is escalated.
3. **Structured/AST merge dissolves a large fraction of textual conflicts before any LLM is invoked** — and should be the first layer. Mergiraf and entity-level "weave" merge eliminate false conflicts git invents; but convergence ≠ correctness.
4. **Verification is the real oracle, and current test suites are dangerously weak at catching semantic merge conflicts** — generated-test detection rates for injected semantic conflicts are only ~32% (SAM: 9/28), and roughly two-thirds of real regression errors are change-interaction errors that isolated testing misses.
5. **Prevention beats resolution.** Cross-agent conflicts occur at roughly double the intra-agent rate (41.7% vs 19.8%, non-overlapping 95% CIs); PR size strongly predicts conflict rate; fine-grained continuous integration and symbol-level coordination shrink the conflict surface.
6. **The economics are governed by a Chow-style threshold**: auto-merge iff estimated success probability p ≥ 1 − c/r, where c is human-review cost and r is bad-merge/revert cost.

### 2. Avenue-by-avenue assessment

**Avenue 1 — Better models/training.** *Proven:* RL (GRPO) gives large parameter-efficiency wins — LLMergeJ at 14B beats Claude Opus 4, o3 Pro, and Grok 4. Retrieval/context augmentation (Rover's multi-layer code property graph) yields single-digit-to-teens percentage similarity gains over standalone LLMs. *Promising:* repo-specific fine-tuning, retrieval of the repo's own past resolutions, and test-time compute (best-of-N, self-consistency; CodeT-style generated-test ranking gave ~7-10% pass@1 gains in adjacent code-gen tasks). *Dead end for the ceiling:* raw scale — commercial-model performance is flat across 11 languages and stuck under 60%. Best-of-N/self-consistency plateau because selection methods fail to surface rare correct samples without a strong verifier (documented saturation in self-improvement studies). Verdict: training buys parameter efficiency and coverage, not a breach of the ceiling.

**Avenue 2 — Calibrated abstention (highest leverage).** The calibrated LLM-as-judge result (100% precision / 64.6% recall on n=292; arXiv:2607.27674) is the key primitive: a judge that never false-accepts, composed with any solver, yields an auto-merge pipeline whose false-accept rate is bounded by the judge's. *Compounding math:* if the solver's correctness on the attempted set is s and the judge has precision ≈1.0 with recall q, the auto-accepted set has near-zero errors at coverage ≈ s·q — e.g., a 55%-accurate solver behind a 100%-precision/65%-recall judge auto-merges ≈36% of true conflicts with almost no bad merges, escalating the rest. Selective-prediction theory (El-Yaniv & Wiener risk-coverage; Geifman & El-Yaniv SelectiveNet) and learning-to-reject (Cortes-DeSalvo-Mohri, ALT 2016) prove confidence-thresholding is suboptimal unless the model is Bayes-optimal — so **train a dedicated "should-escalate" rejector** rather than thresholding raw resolver confidence. This is the most decision-relevant avenue and the one most underexploited by current merge tooling.

**Avenue 3 — Verification instead of trust.** SafeMerge (Sousa-Dillig-Lahiri, OOPSLA 2018) *proves* semantic conflict-freedom for a useful subset (75% of 52 scenarios) but only for same-method edits and does not scale to cross-method interference — TOM (arXiv:2003.00154) reports SafeMerge errored on 17/18 same-method merges in MCon4J and timed out on the rest, while TOM's generated regression tests found 45 conflicting 3-way and 87 conflicting octopus merges. Static interference detection (the Horwitz-Prins-Reps program-integration lineage, modernized) gives F1 ≈ 0.50; RefFilter improves precision by discarding refactoring-induced false positives. **The critical finding is that CI is a weak oracle:** SAM detects only ~32% of injected semantic conflicts (9/28), and Böhme et al. found *"two thirds of such long-standing regressions are introduced due to change interactions,"* with critical-sequence tests 15× more likely to expose the bug and one-third of regressions taking 2+ years to surface. Speculative merge queues (Uber SubmitQueue) are the mature industrial oracle: build-target conflict analysis + a speculation tree over landing orders keeps mainline green across thousands of commits/day, with a 74% wait-time improvement from out-of-order landing. Verdict: verification is where safety actually comes from, but the tooling is immature for the semantic case.

**Avenue 4 — Prevention (strongest evidence base).** Cross-agent conflict rate (41.7%) is ~double intra-agent (19.8%) with non-overlapping 95% CIs (arXiv:2607.04697, 716 replayed merges). In the AgenticFlict dataset (arXiv:2604.03551, 27.67% overall conflict rate), Copilot conflicts least (~15%) and OpenAI Codex most (~32%, more than double Copilot); conflict rate rises with PR size (≈9.9% at 2-line churn → 30%+ at 25 lines). The Specification Gap paper (arXiv:2603.24284) shows a 25-39pp coordination gap that is closed by *restoring full specifications* (recovering the 89% single-agent ceiling), while *conflict reports add no measurable benefit* — implying shared intent, not better merge detection, is what prevents collisions. Pre-write admission (ATM), state management (STORM), and coordination protocols (MPAC) move the conflict boundary earlier. Verdict: the cheapest correctness is the conflict you never create.

**Avenue 5 — Representation-level fixes.** Mergiraf (structured, tree-sitter, 33 languages; 42% fewer false negatives than Spork) and Spork dissolve syntactic false conflicts; entity-level weave merge goes further with unordered-set semantics for class members and imports (jj discussion #8831; 31/31 vs git's 15/31 on a small benchmark). CRDTs guarantee convergence but not correctness — CodeCRDT (arXiv:2510.18893) empirically shows **5-10% residual semantic conflicts despite 100% structural convergence and zero merge failures** across 600 trials. Operation/refactoring-aware merge (IntelliMerge 58.9% conflict reduction; RefMerge 25% of scenarios) composes with LLM resolution as a pre-filter. Verdict: representation removes the *easy* conflicts and should run before any LLM, but it explicitly does not touch the semantic residual.

**Avenue 6 — Reframing the problem.** Agent-authored branches carry artifacts humans never had: prompts, plans, tool traces. **Provenance/intent-conditioned merging is essentially unexplored in the published literature** — a genuine open problem. Regenerate-instead-of-merge is supported indirectly by the Specification Gap result: restoring the full spec recovers the single-agent ceiling, implying regeneration-with-shared-context can beat text merging when specs exist. Semantic-conflict detection is a distinct problem from textual resolution and is the least-solved of all. Verdict: the biggest conceptual upside and the biggest research gap live here.

**Avenue 7 — Systems-level safety design.** Canary/graduated rollout with automatic rollback (Argo Rollouts, AppConfig/CloudWatch patterns; Google SRE canary practice) is the mature transfer pattern — but note the caveat from ML-canary literature that infrastructure telemetry has "no surface area" for behavioral degradation, so merge canaries need behavioral, not just error-rate, signals. The escalation economics reduce to **Chow's reject rule** (Chow 1970): reject/escalate when the top posterior falls below threshold t = (C_reject − C_correct)/(C_error − C_correct); with correct-merge cost ≈0, review cost c, and bad-merge cost r, this is **auto-merge iff p ≥ 1 − c/r**. Learning-to-defer (Mozannar & Sontag, ICML 2020) gives the calibration-robust, learned version. Real magnitudes come from CI cost studies (Gruber et al., ICST 2024): an automated rerun cost $0.0002 while a manual pipeline-failure investigation cost $5.67, and flaky tests consumed ≥2.5% of developer time — concrete inputs for c vs r.

### 3. Recommended layered architecture

The highest-expected-value stack, in execution order:

1. **Prevent** — Decompose tasks to minimize symbol/file overlap; lease/own symbols and subtrees; integrate agent work continuously at fine granularity (small PRs — churn is the dominant conflict predictor); **share a living specification across agents** (closes 25-39pp of the coordination gap, far more than any merge-detection improvement).
2. **Represent** — Line merge → Mergiraf/structured AST merge → entity-level merge; carry jj first-class conflicts so unresolved states don't block downstream progress and can be re-resolved after rebases.
3. **Resolve** — LLM resolves *only* low-risk hunks; augment with retrieval of the repo's own past resolutions and Rover-style dependency context; best-of-N with a verifier, not bare sampling.
4. **Verify** — Compile + existing tests + **generated/differential regression tests** (behavior of merged code vs both parents, à la TOM/SAM) + static interference detection (RefFilter-filtered) + a **speculative merge queue** (SubmitQueue-style) as the landing gate.
5. **Abstain/Escalate** — A calibrated judge (target 100% precision) gates every auto-merge; anything below p ≥ 1 − c/r escalates to human/agent review; canary-merge high-blast-radius changes with automatic revert armed on behavioral signals.

**Residual risk, honestly stated:** Even with all five layers, ~5-10% of structurally clean multi-agent merges harbor semantic conflicts (CodeCRDT), and generated tests catch only about one-third of them (SAM). The unverified residual will reach production unless blast-radius limits and automatic revert are in place. The stack does not eliminate semantic conflicts — it *contains* them and ensures the ones that escape are cheap to revert.

### 4. Open research problems (hand-off ready)
1. **Calibrated semantic-conflict detector with a provable false-negative bound.** Build a detector for "compiles/merges cleanly but breaks behavior" whose miss rate is bounded and certified (e.g., via conformal guarantees), evaluated on a labeled corpus of real semantic merge conflicts. This is the binding constraint on safe automation.
2. **Provenance-conditioned merging.** Formalize and evaluate resolution that conditions on both branches' task specs, plans, and tool traces — does intent provenance raise resolution accuracy above the text-only 60% ceiling, and by how much?
3. **Regeneration-vs-resolution decision theory.** Derive the cost/latency/correctness conditions under which re-running one agent on top of the other's result dominates text merging, and build a policy that chooses per-conflict.
4. **Scalable cross-method semantic conflict-freedom verification.** Extend SafeMerge-style relational verification beyond same-method edits to whole-program interference at CI-compatible latencies.
5. **Distribution-robust calibration of resolver confidence.** Produce confidence estimates good enough to make the Chow threshold (p ≥ 1 − c/r) operational under the distribution shift of agent-generated code — likely via a learned rejector (learning-to-defer) rather than raw model probabilities.

### 5. Maximum safe automation today, and the single most valuable advance
**Maximum safe level with today's published techniques: approximately 40-55% of conflicts auto-resolved at high precision**, achieved by a solver (≈55% developer-match) behind a 100%-precision/~65%-recall judge, further gated by differential testing and a speculative merge queue, with everything below the p ≥ 1 − c/r threshold escalated. This is a *coverage* figure at near-zero false-accept — not an accuracy figure — which is precisely why it is safe.

**The single advance that would raise it most: a calibrated, low-false-negative semantic-conflict detector.** Once prevention and structured/entity-level merge have removed the syntactic and easy cases, the entire residual risk is behavior-breaks-but-compiles conflicts — the 5-10% CodeCRDT residual — which no current verifier reliably catches (SAM ~32%, static interference F1 0.50, SafeMerge same-method only). A detector that could certify "no semantic interference" for a large, well-defined subset would directly convert abstained conflicts into safe auto-merges and push the safe ceiling well past today's ~55%.

## Recommendations
- **Build the abstention/judge layer before investing in a better solver** — it is strictly higher-leverage and turns any mediocre solver into a safe one.
- **Treat CI as a weak oracle.** Fund merge-specific test generation (TOM/SAM-style differential tests) and mutation-gated adequacy on the changed lines; do not trust a green build on a multi-agent merge.
- **Prevent aggressively:** small PRs, symbol leasing/ownership maps, shared living specs, and continuous fine-grained integration — the spec-sharing result shows this beats every post-hoc detection improvement.
- **Adopt structured + entity-level merge (Mergiraf → weave) before LLM resolution**, and keep jj first-class conflicts so partial progress is never blocked.
- **Instrument the p, c, r economics explicitly** and set the auto-merge threshold at p ≥ 1 − c/r; use the CI cost magnitudes ($0.0002 rerun vs $5.67 investigation) as a starting calibration, and re-tune as your revert cost r changes with blast radius.
- **Benchmarks/thresholds that would change the recommendation:** if a resolver credibly clears ~75%+ *verified-correct* (not exact-match) on a real-conflict benchmark, shift budget from abstention toward direct resolution; if a semantic-conflict detector reaches ≥90% recall at high precision, raise the auto-merge coverage target and relax escalation; if cross-agent conflict rate can be driven near the intra-agent 19.8% via coordination, deprioritize resolution investment in favor of prevention.

## Caveats
- **Metric validity:** Exact-match and developer-match metrics *understate* true correctness (semantically equivalent resolutions are scored wrong), while "matches the developer's commit" is *not* ground truth for correctness — the developer may have merged a latent bug. The calibrated-judge floor (~55%) and Merge-Bench source-match (~59-62%) should be read as conservative lower bounds, and the 60% ceiling is likely a modest underestimate of achievable *correct* resolution.
- **Where papers disagree:** AgenticFlict and the AI-PR study report different absolute agent conflict rates (e.g., Codex ~32% vs Cursor ~20%) because of differing corpora and merge-replay methodologies; treat the *relative* ordering (cross-agent ≫ intra-agent, large PR ≫ small PR) as robust and the absolute percentages as corpus-specific.
- **Vendor claims are unverified** (weave's 31/31, Augment/Intent's verifier, MindStudio/Autonoma guidance) and are frequently *throughput* claims, not *correctness* claims — the weave benchmark in particular is 31 hand-built scenarios, not a large real-conflict corpus.
- **CRDT "100% convergence" is a structural guarantee, not a correctness guarantee** — CodeCRDT's own authors flag the 5-10% semantic residual, and it should never be read as a solved-conflict figure.
- The **semantic-conflict detection numbers (SAM 32%, static F1 0.50)** come from small datasets (28-99 units) and may not transfer; they establish that the problem is hard and under-solved, not a precise operating point.
